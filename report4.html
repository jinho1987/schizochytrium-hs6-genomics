Introns & UTRs for intron-mediated enhancement (IME)

A native intron sitting inside the 5′UTR (or very early in the CDS) of a highly-expressed gene is one of the more reliable, underused synthetic-biology parts — splicing deposits an Exon Junction Complex at the splice site that improves nuclear export, translation initiation, and mRNA stability. It's a well-documented effect (maize Ubi1, rice Act1 are the classic examples) that's largely independent of the promoter itself.

StringTie-assembled transcript structures (merged across all 6 RNA-seq libraries) were matched to BRAKER CDS coordinates for 9,068 of the 9,236 stable-expression candidate genes, and searched for exon-exon junctions falling entirely inside the inferred 5′UTR.

False positives caught and removed

The initial scan returned several genes with implausible 12–14 kb "5′UTRs" — almost certainly StringTie merging two adjacent genes into one assembled transcript (read-through/fusion), not real UTR structure. These were filtered out (5′UTR length capped at a generous 1.2 kb) before reporting candidates below.

GO/KEGG enrichment (formal statistical test)

Formal hypergeometric enrichment testing (with FDR correction) on the HS6-vs-#4 significant DE gene set — this had already been run earlier in the project but is included here for completeness since it directly strengthens the interpretation used throughout this report series.

Headline result

The actin/myosin/cytoskeleton signature among genes down in HS6 is extremely significant: GO:0051015 (actin filament binding), 30/291 genes, p.adjust = 1.7×10⁻¹³. This puts real statistical weight behind the "less cytoskeleton, faster growth" interpretation of the HS6 phenotype — not just a descriptive impression from eyeballing gene lists.

Genes up in HS6 show much weaker enrichment (best p.adjust ≈ 0.026: ferric-chelate reductase activity, high-light-intensity response) — a real but modest signal, not comparable in strength to the cytoskeleton finding.

One clear qualitative difference: DNA transposons

Strain #7 carries 134 DNA-transposon copies (mostly PiggyBac-family, 178.6 kb total) that are completely absent from HS6 (0 copies). No TE found at the MVK duplication breakpoint

Checked directly: within ±5 kb of the duplicated MVK locus (g10519, Scaffolds_28:710,505–712,837), the only annotated repeat features are a short unclassified element ~4.4 kb upstream of the gene start and a simple (CAAG)n repeat ~800 bp upstream — neither overlaps the gene body or sits at a plausible breakpoint position. This analysis does not support a TE-mediated mechanism for the Scaffolds_28 MVK duplication; segmental/whole-region duplication (e.g. via replication slippage or an unequal crossover with no TE involvement) remains the more likely explanation given the data on hand. Reported as a negative result rather than omitted, since the original motivation for running this analysis was explicitly to test this hypothesis.

CarveMe's default draft model used a generic, BiGG-derived biomass equation not calibrated to Schizochytrium — it literally included a bacterial peptidoglycan cell-wall precursor (uaagmda_c), which a eukaryotic alga simply doesn't have, and essentially zero lipid weighting despite this being a lipid-accumulation organism (50–77% dry-weight lipid reported in the literature under lipid-accumulation conditions). Revised interpretation — the biomass fix wasn't the actual problem

Fixing the biomass equation left the WT4:HS6 ratio essentially unchanged (~2.1× either way). That's an important, honest correction to the caveat above: the counter-intuitive "HS6 lower than WT4" ordering is not mainly a biomass-equation artifact after all — it more likely reflects a real limitation of this crude E-Flux implementation itself, which caps each reaction's flux at a value scaled by the single highest-expressed gene among its associated genes. That kind of hard, per-reaction ceiling is known to behave unevenly when two conditions have different overall expression-distribution shapes, independent of real metabolic capacity. Net effect: don't trust the absolute growth-objective comparison between strains from this method, with or without the biomass fix. The qualitative pattern — central glycolysis dominates flux capacity in both conditions, with the specific downstream branch differing (glutamate dehydrogenase vs. glycerophosphodiester metabolism) — is the only part worth treating as a weak signal.

A properly useful version of this analysis would need: (1) a real Schizochytrium biomass equation from measured composition data, not a literature-typical approximation, (2) curation of the lipid/DHA-synthesis pathway reactions, which CarveMe's generic bacterial-leaning reaction database does not represent well for a PKS-type DHA synthase system, and (3) a more sophisticated flux-constraint method than linear E-Flux (e.g. GIMME or iMAT, which handle cross-condition expression-scale differences more carefully).

DEG statistical rigor & annotation audit (HS6 vs. #4)

The genotype-effect DE test (1,223 significant genes at padj<0.05, out of 13,529 tested) has been used throughout this project's reports. This section runs the standard rigor checks that hadn't been done yet: is the test itself well-calibrated, how complete is the annotation of what came out significant, what does the effect-size distribution actually look like, and does a proper KEGG pathway-level test back up the lipid-pathway narrative used elsewhere.

P-value calibration

A well-behaved DE test should show a roughly flat (uniform) p-value distribution among non-significant genes, with an excess concentrated near p=0 reflecting real signal. Binning all 13,529 p-values into deciles:

The tail bins (p>0.5, where only non-significant genes should live) are nearly perfectly flat — bin counts range 1,116–1,345 against an expected ~1,253 under a uniform null, a coefficient of variation of just 0.042. That's the signature of a properly-calibrated test, not one artificially inflating significance. The 2.03× enrichment near p=0 is real but modest — consistent with a genuine but not overwhelming biological signal, which is exactly what you'd expect from an n=1 design: real effects are detectable, but the design can't support claiming a huge fraction of the genome is confidently affected.

Read this before the numbers below: draft, scaffold-level assembly changes how to interpret SV calls

The HS6 reference is a scaffold-level draft assembly, not a chromosome-level one. Discordant read-pair signals between different scaffolds (delly's BND/translocation calls) are the classic signature of either a real interchromosomal rearrangement or a scaffold ordering/orientation artifact in the assembly itself — the two are not distinguishable from short-read data alone. Delly found 455 raw BND calls (98 passing its own quality filter), heavily outnumbering every other SV type combined. These are reported for completeness but are not individually interpreted as confirmed rearrangements below — that would overclaim what scaffold-level short-read data can actually support. The intra-scaffold calls (DEL/DUP/INV, particularly the PRECISE/split-read-confirmed ones) are on much firmer ground and are the focus of this section.

This is a single-caller result — the project's own prior standard required two

report2.html §04 records that the sequencing vendor already ran a three-tool SV screen (BreakDancer, Pindel, Delly) on this same strain pair and required agreement from ≥2 methods before calling anything confirmed — and found nothing that cleared that bar. This section's 421kb inversion and 41.7kb deletion come from Delly alone, run fresh for this survey; they have not been cross-checked against BreakDancer or Pindel, and are exactly the kind of single-caller signal the vendor's stricter standard was designed to filter out. That doesn't make them wrong — Delly's 646-read-pair support on the inversion is unusually strong, and the deletion is split-read/PRECISE — but it does mean they should be held to the same bar as everything else on this site: real until the junction PCR below says otherwise, not before.

Independent check: is this an HS6 assembly artifact, or real? Two lines of evidence say assembly artifact is now unlikely

The caveat above leaves two possible explanations for the two headline calls: a real difference between WT4 and HS6, or a misassembly in the HS6 reference itself that Delly's short-read mapping is picking up as a false discordant signal. Both can be checked directly using data already on hand at SKKU — the HS6 genome was assembled four independent ways from the same underlying PacBio HiFi reads (flye, hifiasm, masurca, nextdenovo, all in working_files/2.assembly/), and the raw HiFi reads themselves are archived separately from this project folder.

1. Cross-assembly consistency: 3 independent assemblers agree with the reference at both loci

The sequence flanking each breakpoint was aligned (minimap2) against all four independently-assembled genomes. At the 421kb inversion, flye and masurca both place the two breakpoint-flanking regions on the same single contig, in the same relative order and at the same ~420kb spacing as the hifiasm-derived reference — i.e., three separate assembly algorithms, built purely from HS6's own reads with no WT4 data involved, agree on this locus's architecture (nextdenovo split the region across two contigs and is uninformative here, neither confirming nor contradicting). 2. Direct long-read support: HS6's own raw HiFi reads span every breakpoint cleanly

HS6's raw PacBio HiFi reads (1.9GB, archived separately from this project folder) were aligned directly against the reference (minimap2 map-hifi). Coverage at all four breakpoint positions is normal (37–50×, matching the genome-wide average of 38×) — no dropouts, no discontinuities. Between 41 and 75 individual long reads span each exact breakpoint coordinate with a single continuous alignment, not a split one. Checking specifically for split/supplementary alignments (the direct long-read signature of a real misassembly) turned up exactly one, out of dozens of reads covering that locus, and its CIGAR string is riddled with dozens of scattered small indels — the signature of one read struggling through a repetitive stretch, not an independent corroborating signal.

Can a non-coding variant change the adjacent gene's protein fold? No — but here's the mechanism that's actually plausible

Worth stating precisely, since it's easy to conflate with the structural work above: translation reads the mRNA's coding sequence codon-by-codon, so if a variant never touches that sequence, the resulting protein — and its fold — is necessarily identical. This is a hard mechanistic boundary, not an untested possibility. It rules out any "folding shift" for the four intergenic candidates (g6124, g568, g8342, g8869) by construction: same protein, only the expression level differs.

The one case where a structural argument is even worth checking is g14345, since its variant sits inside the gene (intronic). Introns are transcribed before being spliced out, so a variant there could in principle produce a genuinely different protein isoform — but only by disrupting canonical splice-site recognition (the GT…AG boundaries and branch point), not by any other route. Checked directly against g14345's exon structure: the variant (Scaffolds_4:2,843,502) sits 134bp from the nearest 5′ donor site and 172bp from the 3′ acceptor, squarely in the intron's interior rather than near either canonical splice boundary or the branch-point zone (typically only 18–40bp upstream of the acceptor). Splicing disruption is therefore not well-supported for this variant specifically.

A better-fitting mechanism already established elsewhere on this site: intron-mediated enhancement (IME)

§2 of this report already documents native introns boosting transcript output in this organism through spliceosome-associated recruitment — a real mechanism that changes expression level, not splice-site fidelity or protein sequence. That fits g14345 far better than a splicing-disruption story: the gene is up in HS6 (log2FC=+0.76), exactly the direction an altered IME element would produce, and requires no change to the protein at all. This remains a hypothesis, not a confirmed mechanism — distinguishing "true IME change" from "linked to some other regulatory element nearby" needs the same RT-PCR-across-the-intron test already proposed for the splicing question.

The interface lands beside the catalytic cleft, not inside it — and that's a plausible result, not a failure

Only 2 of the 31 catalytic-motif residues (288–289, at the edge of the HRD-like loop) fall within the interface; the catalytic-region centroid sits 31.8Å from the ligand's centroid, and the single closest catalytic-to-ligand approach is 8.0Å (residue 289 to RPS6's residue 107) — close, but not direct engagement. The interface instead concentrates at 246–257, 381–386, and the C-terminal region (423–444).

Lightdock searches the entire molecular surface with no prior knowledge of "where the active site is," so this isn't a targeted-and-missed result. It's consistent with a real, well-documented feature of kinase–substrate biology: many kinases (ribosomal S6 kinases included) use a substrate-docking groove physically separate from the catalytic cleft to first recognize a substrate, which then transiently loops into the active site for the phosphotransfer step itself — a interaction geometry blind docking is well-suited to find, and the catalytic step itself is not. Read as "a plausible recognition-type interface," not "the catalytic engagement pose."

What this test does and doesn't establish about HS6 vs. #4

Restating the caveat from above, now with the completed result: g6124 has zero sequence differences of any kind between HS6 and #4 — not just at the CDS level (0 SNPs, verified even under the most permissive no-filter variant-calling setting), but genome-wide for this gene, because g6124 is a single-exon gene with no introns to carry a non-coding variant either. The nearest variation at all is 2 intergenic SNPs sitting between g6124 and its neighbor g6125 — not confirmed to be in either gene's own regulatory region specifically.

So this docking result cannot be, and was never testing for, a structural difference between the two genotypes: there is only one g6124 structure, shared identically by both strains. What it does establish is that the eggNOG "ribosomal S6 kinase" annotation is structurally credible — the fold has the expected bilobal kinase architecture with intact catalytic motifs, and it forms a convergent, non-random docking interface with this organism's own RPS6. That's supporting evidence the annotation itself is trustworthy, which matters for interpreting g6124's differential expression (log2FC=−0.59, padj=0.017) as biologically meaningful rather than a mis-annotated gene — not evidence of a mutation-driven structural change.

Worth keeping in view alongside g6423: it shows a larger effect size (log2FC=−1.11, padj=0.019) than g6124 does, with comparable significance. g6124 was chosen for this structural test because it has a tractable, well-characterized native substrate to dock against — not because the expression data marks it as the strongest of the three original candidate genes (g6423, g1760, g6124). g1760, for reference, does not clear significance after correction at all (padj=0.257).

Known tool-install gotchas hit during this work (documented so they don't cost you the same time)

  • RepeatModeler uses -pa for parallel jobs, not -threads — the wrong flag causes it to print usage and silently produce no output, which then makes RepeatMasker fail downstream with a confusing "database does not exist" error.
  • eggNOG-mapper's download_eggnog_data.py hardcodes a dead domain (eggnogdb.embl.de) in two of its three download URLs; the working domain is eggnog5.embl.de (already used correctly for the third URL in the same file). Patch with: sed -i 's|eggnogdb.embl.de|eggnog5.embl.de|g' download_eggnog_data.py.
  • bioconda's eggnog-mapper package resolves to an ancient 2.0.1 build (python2.7, no --itype flag) unless pinned; pinning an exact recent version then conflicts with biopython in the conda solver. Installing via pip install eggnog-mapper==2.1.13 into a conda env with just diamond+hmmer sidesteps both problems.
  • CarveMe's model gene IDs are SBML-sanitized (g10740_t1, underscore not dot) — strip the _t\d+ suffix before matching against a plain gene-ID expression table, or every E-Flux constraint silently evaluates to zero and the model collapses to near-zero growth for every condition identically.
